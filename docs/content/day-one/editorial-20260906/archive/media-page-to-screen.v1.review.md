## Q1. What distinguishes a codex from a scroll?

A. Writing on both sides
B. Separate leaves bound along one edge **correct**
C. Text arranged in columns
D. Writing on papyrus

NIV: Revelation 5:1; context REV.5.1-7.
Paid hint: Think of turning pages instead of unrolling a long strip.

A codex joins leaves along an edge in book form. Double-sided writing, columns and papyrus do not themselves define that structure. The vision explicitly describes a scroll written on both sides, which helps eliminate a tempting alternative. Revelation concerns the Lamb’s worthiness to open the sealed scroll; it is not a bookbinding history lesson.

**unapproved:** The passage rules out double-sided writing as the defining distinction without naming a codex. The hint adds how the object is handled. Historical scrolls were commonly written on one side; do not turn that custom into an absolute. Book format belongs to Media publishing scope.

## Q2. Which note in a finely printed book may identify its printer, paper and typeface?

A. An index
B. An epigraph
C. A glossary
D. A colophon **correct**

NIV: Romans 16:22; context ROM.16.21-27.
Paid hint: Look near the end for details about how the book was made.

A colophon can record production details and those responsible for making a book. Tertius identifies himself as the person who wrote down Paul’s letter; this supplies an analogy to a production credit distinct from authorship. The greeting is not called a modern colophon in the question or explanation. An index locates topics, an epigraph supplies an opening quotation, and a glossary explains terms.

**unapproved:** Specific scribal-credit connection, but the label requires book knowledge. The position hint narrows index versus epigraph less than a novice may need; benefit unmeasured. LOC indexed glossary supported the details but its full-page open failed; accessible ABAA and Folger supply the factual basis.

Sources: [source 1](https://www.abaa.org/glossary/colophon), [source 2](https://folgerpedia.folger.edu/Glossary_of_book_history_terms), [source 3](https://guides.loc.gov/russell-maret/glossary).

## Q5. Which household objects can a Foley artist use for a convincing clip-clop of hooves?

A. A bag of dried rice shaken gently
B. Two hollow coconut halves tapped in rhythm **correct**
C. A sheet of cellophane crumpled slowly
D. A piece of canvas rubbed over slats

NIV: Judges 5:22; context JDG.5.19-27.
Paid hint: The hard hollow shells make short, resonant knocks.

Coconut halves are a traditional hoofbeat effect. Rice and other small grains can suggest rain, cellophane can suggest crackling, and canvas over slats can make wind effects. The song of Deborah evokes galloping hoofbeats during battle. This is only an acoustic analogy; no claim that the biblical sound was artificial.

All options are believable sound-making props, but factual correctness does not resolve weak added inference. Also overlaps live-stage sound-effect territory; replace before counting ready.
