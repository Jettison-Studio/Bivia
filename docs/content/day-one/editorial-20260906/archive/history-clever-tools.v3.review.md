## Q1. In traditional letterpress printing, which part of the type carries ink onto the paper?

A. The recessed grooves
B. The raised letter faces **correct**
C. The flat metal backing
D. The spaces between letters

NIV: Job 38:14; context JOB.38.12-21
Paid hint: Imagine which parts of an inked rubber stamp touch the paper.

Letterpress prints from raised, inked surfaces. Recessed ink belongs to intaglio; backing and spaces are not the intended printing surfaces. Job compares the emerging detail of dawn to a seal shaping clay, within God’s challenge to Job. The comparison concerns an impression, not an ancient prediction of printing.

**coordinator_editorial_reviewed_awaiting_verification_and_playtest:** A seal can itself be recessed, so do not claim Job establishes the raised geometry of letterpress. Test whether the stamp analogy helps beyond the seal reading.

Sources: [source 1](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/what-is-print), [source 2](https://printinghistory.org/timeline/)

## Q2. According to Sarah Bradford’s biography, who used “Go Down, Moses” to identify herself to people escaping slavery?

A. Sojourner Truth
B. Harriet Beecher Stowe

NIV: Exodus 8:1–3; context EXO.8.1-7
Paid hint: She was known as a conductor on the Underground Railroad.

Bradford’s 1886 biography describes Tubman singing the spiritual so that people waiting in hiding would recognize their returning guide. The song draws on Moses’ demand to Pharaoh; the displayed passage contains that demand. All four choices were women involved in opposing slavery, but Tubman is the person in this account. The question attributes this reported episode to its source rather than generalizing that spirituals routinely encoded escape directions.

Conductor clue is familiar but may repeat what the stem already implies; benefit remains unmeasured. ## Q3. Why was straw mixed into traditional sun-dried mud bricks?

A. To waterproof the finished bricks
B. To help limit cracking as they dried **correct**
C. To make the brick surfaces smoother
D. To make each brick heavier

NIV: Exodus 5:6-8; context EXO.5.6-14
Paid hint: Small fibres help a drying lump hold together.

Straw helps mud bricks shrink more uniformly as they dry, limiting cracking. It does not waterproof the brick, smooth its surface, or increase its density. Pharaoh increases the burden on enslaved workers by withdrawing supplied straw while retaining their brick quota. The useful material detail is part of that oppression, not an endorsement of it.

**coordinator_editorial_reviewed_awaiting_verification_and_playtest:** Easy opener candidate. Do not generalize to permanent reinforcing strength: NPS specifically distinguishes more uniform shrinkage from added long-term strength. Scripture names the ingredient but not its drying function.

Sources: [source 1](https://www.nps.gov/orgs/1739/upload/preservation-brief-05-adobe.pdf), [source 2](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1981-14-90)

## Q4. On wooden ships, what was oakum used for?

A. Padding the sail edges
B. Polishing the deck boards
C. Filling seams between planks **correct**
D. Binding the anchor to its chain

NIV: Genesis 6:14; context GEN.6.13-22
Paid hint: Think of the gaps through which seawater could enter a hull.

Oakum consists of rope fibres driven into plank seams; pitch then helps seal the joints against water. The alternatives are other conceivable uses of fibrous material, not the documented caulking role. Genesis specifies coating the ark with pitch inside and out. It does not mention oakum or identify the later shipbuilding technique.

**coordinator_editorial_reviewed_awaiting_verification_and_playtest:** Concrete waterproofing analogy, not proof Noah used oakum. Keep pitch and fibres distinct. Hint adds the likely entry point for water without using the correct answer wording.

Sources: [source 1](https://emuseum.aberdeencity.gov.uk/objects/43329/caulking-iron-used-by-the-donor-when-he-was-employed-at-the), [source 2](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-69366)

## Q5. Why did many Roman aqueducts follow winding routes around hills?

A. To keep the water shaded
B. To let boats pass through the channel
C. To raise water pressure at every bend
D. To maintain a gentle downhill gradient **correct**

NIV: Isaiah 40:3-5; context ISA.40.1-8
Paid hint: The water usually travelled without a pump.

Gravity-fed aqueduct channels needed a suitable downward gradient, which could require contour-following routes. Historic England records the winding aqueduct to Dorchester as an example. Such channels were not navigation canals; shade or unaided uphill flow does not explain the required profile. Isaiah announces comfort and a highway for the Lord with transformed terrain; it is not an aqueduct design manual.

**coordinator_revision_required:** Pairing needs review: terrain obstacles and leveling provide the connection, while downhill flow is an added historical fact. Isaiah’s straight highway must not be asserted to describe Roman winding channels. Inverted siphons can cross valleys under pressure, so do not claim every segment of every aqueduct always descends.

Sources: [source 1](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1002730)
