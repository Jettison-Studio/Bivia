## Q2. A soloist sings a short phrase, then a choir answers it. Which musical pattern is this?

A. A canon
B. Call and response **correct**
C. Unison singing
D. A drone

NIV: Psalm 24:8-10; context PSA.24.1-10.
Paid hint: The reply may stay the same even when the leader’s phrase changes.

Call and response alternates a musical call with a reply. A canon overlaps staggered entries of a melody; unison joins the same line; a drone sustains a pitch. Psalm 24 presents questions about the King of glory and answers identifying the Lord. This is a question-and-answer textual analogy, not proof of a particular ancient performance arrangement.

**See coordinator update above:** Q1 concerns a returning section, Q2 alternating participants. Both are easy forms and may feel repetitive; test variety. Hint adds a fixed-response variant but may have little value after the direct question.

Sources: [source 1](https://www.carnegiehall.org/Education/Programs/Link-Up/National-Program/The-Orchestra-Swings/Communicating-Through-Swing).

## Q3. How can a trumpeter change notes without pressing different valves?

A. By covering tone holes
B. By adjusting a reed
C. By moving a slide
D. By changing the lip vibration **correct**

NIV: 1 Corinthians 14:7-8; context 1CO.14.6-12.
Paid hint: The same length of brass tubing supports a series of natural notes.

A player’s vibrating lips excite different resonances in the trumpet’s harmonic series with the same tube length. Valves provide additional tube lengths but are not needed for every note change. Tone holes, reeds and a playing slide describe mechanisms associated with other wind instruments. Paul uses distinct notes and a recognizable trumpet call to illustrate intelligible communication that builds up the church.
