## Q1. What creates the floating figure in the classic Pepper’s Ghost stage illusion?

A. An image projected onto drifting mist
B. A hidden figure reflected in angled glass **correct**
C. A costume glowing under ultraviolet light
D. A painted figure revealed by a rotating screen

NIV: 1 Corinthians 13:12; context 1CO.13.8-13
Paid hint: The audience can look through the same surface that reflects another image.

Angled transparent glass can reflect a lit figure hidden from direct view while allowing the audience to see the stage beyond. Mist projection, ultraviolet effects and rotating scenery are other theatrical techniques. Paul contrasts a present reflected view with future face-to-face knowledge; the reflection detail supports the optical connection without turning his point about incomplete knowledge into an illusion claim.

## Q2. A stage version of this scene could make Haman’s confidence funny through which device?

A. Foreshadowing
B. Slapstick
C. Dramatic irony **correct**
D. Direct address

NIV: Esther 6:1-10; context EST.6.1-10
Paid hint: The audience has heard the king ask about Mordecai before Haman imagines his own reward.

Dramatic irony creates an information gap between audience and character. The reader knows the king has been considering Mordecai’s unrewarded service while Haman assumes that the honor must be for himself. This is a narrative example of the device; Esther is not asserted to have been written as a stage play. Foreshadowing points ahead, slapstick uses physical comedy, and direct address speaks to the audience.

## Q4. Which theatrical device best fits staging the rich man’s deliberation in verses 17–19?

A. A dialogue
B. A soliloquy **correct**
C. A choral response
D. Third-person narration

NIV: Luke 12:16–21; context LUK.12.13-21
Paid hint: Hamlet’s “To be, or not to be” is a famous example.

A soliloquy gives an audience access to a character’s own thoughts through speech. The rich man works through his plans by addressing himself, which fits that device in a proposed stage adaptation. Dialogue exchanges speech between characters; a choral response comes from a group; third-person narration describes a character from outside. The surrounding parable challenges confidence in stored wealth; it is not advice endorsing his plans.

**unapproved_repair:** The reading supplies who addresses whom; the stem does not restate the definition. All four choices are real presentation forms. Exclude the overlapping broader term monologue and the potentially ambiguous aside. The Hamlet example is an independent clue whose benefit is unmeasured. Vocabulary question, not a hard mechanism question. Distinct from the existing dramatic-irony example, but two theater-device questions warrant variety review.
